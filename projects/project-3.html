After 7 years, the system was mature. The operating model wasn't

Canvas, Scotiabank's design system, had grown from a centralized asset library into the foundation beneath every digital product. But governance, awareness, education, and contribution hadn't scaled with it. It needed to become self-sustaining, able to grow reach without growing headcount.

Our organization needed to understand how to leverage and own the system for their benefit.

The human dimension: cost of design system drift

CX gaps as a result of design system drift have a human cost. A button that behaves one way in payments and another way in settings doesn't feel like inconsistency, it feels like unreliability. Multiple small drifts compound into eroded trust.

  • Reducing rework and defects is an investment in quality, less rework means more time spent on solving product problems.
  • Increasing design system adoption means more reach, consistent foundations leading to better digital experiences.

As Canvas matured, design systems team's work shifted from building to curating, and a gap surfaced

Fewer resources, broader reach, constant delivery pressure and competing priorities meant squads quietly drifted from the standards.

The problem wasn't the system itself. It was the operating model around it.

Three big challenges

  • Executive alignment, no systemic change without it
  • Process fragmentation, every business line had its own rhythm
  • A contribution model no one understood, and therefore no one used

Goals

  • Improve organizational communication, updates, and public perception
  • Establish a clear baseline for standards, articulate the operational value of the system
  • Define a scalable self-support model with guidance, tools, and governance

How we solved it

Design craft stance: clarifying ownership at the atomic level

The pivotal decision wasn't a design one, it was political. Using Atomic Design as the scaffolding (foundations → atoms → molecules → organisms → templates → proprietary patterns), we assigned distinct accountability at each level. I brought engineering, product, and design leads from multiple squads to the table and walked out with explicit agreements. That clarification cut rework by 15% in the first cycles.

Alignment & governance

  • Authored white paper for executive leadership around four pillars: education, design & build, community support, communications & governance.
  • Onboarding for new hires.
  • Design Systems check negotiated into every squad's Definition of Done.
  • Team identity repositioned from librarians (asset managers) to architects (platform stewards).

Communications & support

  • Structured touchpoints: 1-on-1s, consultations, accessibility reviews, open feedback forums.
  • Regularized updates across Confluence, Slack, and Teams.

Adoption & contribution

  • Simplified contribution path.
  • Ownership codified via Atomic Design, from foundations through proprietary patterns.

Tension story: the white paper got rejected, and it deserved to

The first draft was written for designers, not business leaders. Tech leadership told us so, directly. In hindsight, they were right, we rebuilt it with business metrics as the spine: rework cost, deployment delay, inconsistency as customer risk.

That experience permanently changed how I write for executives, and I learned an important lesson: take the hard feedback, but don't lose the conviction, reframe the problem from your audience POV.

The result wasn't cleaner libraries, it was a stronger operating model

It proved three things:

  • Design can scale without scaling headcount when governance is in place
  • Autonomy and standards don't compete. They reinforce each other
