Designing a design team: scaling a practice, not just headcount

ROLES: Team leader / Community builder / Coach
Context Challenge Approach Impact

The team had grown fast. The practice hadn't kept up

Scotia Digital's design org had scaled into a matrixed structure running dozens of concurrent projects without a shared vision, skills baseline or growth path.

Designers wanted alignment on purpose, craft, and how we worked together.

The human dimension: designers are users too

  • A quarter of the community were juniors with no clear path forward. Anxiety was high, expectations were unclear.
  • Seniors had become islands, skilled but isolated, duplicating each other's work across squads.
  • Research, content, visual, and service designers had it worst, product designers were the majority, and everyone else's role definition was fuzzy.

What leadership needed from me

To reshape the design function's culture and organizational impact. The organization had hired designers across every experience level fast, without a succession plan or a structured talent pipeline behind the growth.

This wasn't an HR problem. It was a talent and culture problem. We needed a shared model that built transparency and trust, and articulated what it actually meant to be a product designer at Scotia.

The goals we set

  • Ramp-up, faster. Get new designers to meaningful contribution in weeks, not quarters.
  • A craft baseline across every discipline. Product, research, content, service, visual; one bar, expressed differently per practice.
  • Creative confidence + business literacy. Designers who could defend craft and read a P&L document.
  • Stretch-ready juniors. Progressive challenges with the support to grow into them.

Design craft stance: defining and measuring skills

We built a skills matrix framework, the first iteration was an over-engineered artifact: 40+ skills across 5 levels. It was hard to use for self-assessment.

We went back to first principles: what is this actually for? The answer: for a designer to have an honest conversation with their manager about where they are and where they want to go.

So we cut it to the 16 skills that actually differentiated career levels, rewritten in a designer's voice. The second version hit 3× higher self-assessment completion, and we adapted the framework for every discipline in the community.

What we built: a Design Community of Practice (DCoP)

A DCoP runs parallel to day-to-day product operations. It's where craft lives when the squads are heads-down on delivery.

Ours grew from ~10 to 150+ members over 8 years, built on shared knowledge, curiosity, and mutual accountability for craft. It dismantled silos and helped us cultivate excellence through mutual learning opportunities and experiences, peer support and coaching.

Structure

  • A multi-disciplinary model with clear role definitions.
  • Product designers embedded in squads.
  • Research, service and content shared across squads to maximize reach.
  • Visual designers concentrated on design system.

Career ladders

  • Transparent promotion criteria.
  • Expectations defined by experience and sphere of influence.
  • Parallel tracks for people managers and IC leads, so staying in craft was never a demotion.
  • Separate curricula for leadership skills and craft development.

Craft coaching & community rituals

  • Senior–junior mentoring pairs.
  • Cross-squad portfolio reviews.
  • Design jams and critique culture that made craft visible across the organization, not just inside individual squads

Executive influence

  • Positioned design as the voice of the customer at every decision table.
  • Translated design value into business language.
  • Built durable partnerships with Business, Product, and Engineering around shared customer-centric outcomes.

Tension story: transparency vs. manager judgment

When we published promotion criteria for the design ladder, some peer leaders pushed back. They felt it constrained their ability to make judgment calls on their own teams. My argument was the opposite: transparency doesn't replace judgment, it creates the baseline that makes judgment trusted.

We ran a two-quarter pilot, using the criteria alongside HR promotion justifications. Intuitive and criteria-based assessments aligned 90% of the time, enough to convert the skeptics. The 10% that diverged? Those became highly valuable talent conversations for the leadership group.

Culture scaled intentionally and sustainably

  • Design stopped being the bottleneck.
  • Velocity became consistent across squads.
  • Retention and growth outpaced peer technology functions.
  • Senior designers were steadily promoted into leadership.

Platforms design team:

  • From 2 to 20 designers in 6 years.
  • 50% promoted internally into senior roles.
  • Diverse industry backgrounds accelerated adaptability and creative confidence.
